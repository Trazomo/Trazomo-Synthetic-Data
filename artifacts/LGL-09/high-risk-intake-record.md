## 1. Purpose and Standard Applied

This record documents the client due diligence performed on a prospective client that triggered every enhanced-review flag in the firm's Client Acceptance and Financial Crime Policy (the "Policy"). It is written to support a defensible decision under Model Rule 1.16(a) and 1.2(d), and to preserve the reasoning if the firm is later asked why it did or did not act.

The firm is not a financial institution subject to the Bank Secrecy Act and has no suspicious activity reporting obligation. That is not the operative constraint. Three other constraints are:

1. **Model Rule 1.2(d) and ABA Formal Opinion 491.** A lawyer may not counsel or assist a client in conduct the lawyer knows is criminal or fraudulent, and where the facts known to the lawyer establish a high probability that the client seeks to use the lawyer's services for such conduct, the lawyer has a duty to inquire further. A high composite score under the Policy is the firm's operational proxy for that duty. Unresolved high-probability indicators mean the engagement cannot be accepted while they remain unresolved.
2. **Sanctions law.** The prohibitions administered by the U.S. Department of the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control bind the firm as a U.S. person, including the prohibition on dealing in blocked property and the treatment of entities owned 50 percent or more, directly or indirectly, by blocked persons. Providing legal services to a blocked person, or receiving property in which a blocked person has an interest, is a strict-liability exposure. A legal-services general license is not assumed and none has been sought.
3. **The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act.** Where the counterparty is a state-owned enterprise and the transaction contemplates local intermediaries, the firm's own conduct and its advice are within the anti-bribery and books-and-records provisions.

The Policy's scoring model is described in Part 6. The maximum possible score is 15. This file scored 15.

The Policy does not apply a time-decay to former PEP status automatically. Under Policy Section 7.4, a former PEP remains in scope where the source of wealth accumulated during or immediately after the period of public function, where the person retains influence in the relevant sector, or where the proposed matter is in the same sector the person previously regulated. **All three conditions are met here.** The matter is an energy transaction in a sector Mr. Kavaris regulated as deputy minister and in which he chaired the state-owned incumbent, and his separation from public office is four years, well inside the Policy's five-year look-back for sector-adjacent matters.

### 6.1 F1, client type and ownership transparency: 3 of 3

The registered holder is a nominee. Beneficial ownership at tiers 2 and 3 cannot be verified against any public register and rests on documents produced by the client's own agents, one of which is redacted at the beneficiary class. The prospective client is a shell with no employees, no premises, and no financial statements. A son of the ultimate beneficial owner sits on the board alongside two corporate directors supplied by the same trust company that provides the registered office. Under Policy Table 7.1, a nominee holder combined with two or more secrecy-jurisdiction layers scores 3 without further analysis.

### 6.2 F2, jurisdiction, matter type, and corruption exposure: 3 of 3

The ownership chain runs BVI to Seychelles to Cayman Islands. The target assets are located in a third jurisdiction. The seller of the 55 percent interest is Verkhal National Energy Company, a state-owned enterprise whose supervisory board the ultimate beneficial owner chaired until 2021. Its officers are foreign officials for FCPA purposes.

The requesting attorney's intake note records that the client "has a local relationship consultant who will handle approvals" and that the consultant's fee is "success-based and outside the transaction documents." That is the classic pattern the FCPA's anti-bribery and books-and-records provisions are aimed at, and the firm would be asked to paper a transaction whose regulatory approvals are being procured by an intermediary the firm cannot diligence.

### 6.3 F3, politically exposed person exposure: 3 of 3

See Part 4. Former senior foreign PEP; the matter is in the sector the PEP regulated; the counterparty is the state-owned enterprise the PEP chaired; a PEP family member is a director of the prospective client. Under Policy Table 7.4, any one of the last three conditions scores 3 on its own.

### 6.4 F4, sanctions screening outcome: 3 of 3

An unresolved potential SDN match at 94.1 similarity on the ultimate beneficial owner, plus a 50 percent rule exposure on the transaction counterparty. Policy Section 8.2 provides that an unresolved potential match on a beneficial owner scores 3 and blocks acceptance regardless of the composite. This factor alone is dispositive.

### 6.5 F5, source of funds and payment channel: 3 of 3

On July 24, 2026 the firm's trust account received a wire of **USD 850,000** described as an advance flat fee for the engagement. The wire has three defects:

1. **Third-party payor.** The originator was Larkfield Nominees (Mauritius) Ltd., not the prospective client and not any entity in the disclosed ownership chain. No explanation of the relationship was provided before the wire arrived.
2. **Offshore routing without documentation.** The funds were routed from a private bank in Mauritius through a correspondent, with an originator-to-beneficiary reference of "PROJECT MERIDIAN ADVISORY." The firm holds no account-opening documentation, no evidence of the payor's beneficial ownership, and no source-of-wealth narrative for the funds.
3. **Structure of the fee.** An advance flat fee of USD 850,000 for a matter that has not been scoped, with the client requesting in writing on July 24, 2026 that any unused balance be refunded to "an account to be nominated," which is an account other than the originating account.

The requesting attorney did not solicit this payment and the client was not invited to fund in advance. Funds arriving before an engagement letter, from a payor who is not the client, to be refunded to a third account, is a movement-of-value pattern rather than a fee payment. Under Policy Section 9.3 the funds were placed on hold in the client trust account on July 24, 2026 and were not applied to any fee. See Part 9 for disposition.

## 8. Recommendation

**DECLINE.** The recommendation does not rest on the composite score alone. It rests on three independent grounds, any one of which is sufficient:

1. The potential SDN match on the ultimate beneficial owner is unresolved and cannot be resolved on the documents the client is willing to produce. Acting without resolving it risks a strict-liability sanctions violation.
2. The engagement would place the firm in a transaction with a state-owned counterparty where regulatory approvals are being handled by an undisclosed intermediary on a success fee. The firm cannot advise on, or paper, a transaction whose approval mechanism it is not permitted to see.
3. The advance payment is a third-party offshore wire with a requested refund to a different account. Accepting it and later returning it to a nominated account would make the firm's trust account a transmission channel.

Conditional acceptance was considered and rejected. Conditions can cure information gaps; they cannot cure a client's refusal to close them.
